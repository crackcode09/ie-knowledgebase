# Grinding Scrap: Rolling Out the Line 3 Fix

## Approve $38,000 to cut grinding scrap in half on Lines 1, 2 and 4
- Line 3 scrap fell from 4.5% to 2.0%
- Same fix on three more lines: about $250,000 a year
- Payback in about two months
<!-- I'm asking for one decision today: thirty-eight thousand dollars to put the Line 3 fix on Lines 1, 2 and 4. On Line 3, scrap went from four and a half percent to two percent and has stayed there for thirteen weeks. On three more lines we expect about a quarter of a million dollars a year, and the money comes back in about two months. Let me show you why we're confident. -->

## Line 3 scrap has stayed near 2% for 13 weeks
![Weekly scrap, before and after](../figures/05_scrap_after.png)
<!-- This is weekly scrap. Before the change, it ran around four and a half percent. Since week fourteen, it has held around two percent. The drop is far bigger than the week-to-week noise, so this is not luck. -->

## Two defects made 70% of the scrap: oversize and burn
![Defect Pareto](../figures/05_defect_pareto.png)
<!-- When we sorted the scrapped parts, two defects stood out. Parts ground too large, and parts with burn marks. Together they were seventy percent of all scrap. So we went after the causes of those two. -->

## Both came from coolant drift and too-infrequent wheel dressing
- Coolant mix drifted from 3% to 8% (target 5%)
- Wheel was dressed every 60 to 150 parts, depending on the operator
<!-- Two things were drifting. The coolant mix should be five percent, but we measured anywhere from three to eight. And each operator dressed the wheel on their own schedule, from every sixty to every hundred fifty parts. Weak coolant and a dull wheel make heat, and heat makes oversize parts and burn. -->

## Three changes now hold the process steady
- A pump keeps the coolant mix at 5%
- The machine dresses the wheel every 40 parts
- A gauge checks each part and corrects the machine
<!-- We made three changes. A proportioning pump holds the coolant at five percent. The machine now counts parts and dresses the wheel every forty. And an in-process gauge measures every part and nudges the machine back if it starts to drift. None of these depend on someone remembering. -->

## The same parts and causes exist on Lines 1, 2 and 4
- Same machines, coolant system and dressing practice
- Risk: Line 4 runs a different wheel; we will pilot it first
- Owner: process engineering; done in 10 weeks
<!-- The other three lines use the same machines and the same coolant setup, and dress the wheel the same way, so we expect the same result. The one difference is Line 4, which runs a different wheel, so we'll pilot there for two weeks before committing. Process engineering owns this, and we'll be done in ten weeks. -->

## Decision needed today: approve $38,000
<!-- So, the decision: thirty-eight thousand dollars for about two hundred fifty thousand a year. I'm happy to take questions. The full analysis, including the statistics and the control plan, is in the appendix. -->

## Appendix A: Statistics and capability
- Scrap reduction 2.47 points, 95% CI 2.1 to 2.8; Welch t-test p < 0.001
- Coolant concentration vs diameter deviation: R-sq 0.62
- Diameter Cpk 0.71 before, 1.41 after; gauge R&R 11.8% of tolerance
