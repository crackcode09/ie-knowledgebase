# Module 02 — Defining the Objectives of the Presentation

## Objectives

- Analyze an audience: who decides, what they know, what they care about.
- Treat the audience as a customer and capture their "voice".
- Write a purpose statement and a one-sentence message before building any slides.

## Start with the audience, not the slides

**Audience analysis** answers six questions:

| Question | Line 3 rollout example |
|---|---|
| Who is in the room, and who **decides**? | Plant manager (decides), finance manager, operations manager, quality manager, maintenance manager |
| What do they already know? | That Line 3 had a scrap problem; not the cause or fix |
| What do they care about? | Cost, capacity, customer complaints, safety, effort for their teams |
| What might worry them? | "Will it work on the other lines?" "Who does the work?" "Is the saving real?" |
| What is their attention budget? | 10 minutes on a crowded agenda |
| How do they prefer information? | Plant manager: bottom line first. Finance: wants to see the savings math. |

## The audience is your customer

The voice-of-the-customer methods used for products apply to presentations. Before you build, **ask** a couple of audience members (or their assistants or peers):

- "What would make this worth your 10 minutes?"
- "What would you need to see to approve it?"
- "What concerns have you heard about this?"

Turn the answers into requirements — the presentation's **critical-to-quality (CTQ) characteristics**:

| Voice of the audience | Requirement for the presentation |
|---|---|
| "Just tell me what you need." | The ask on slide 1, with amount and payback |
| "Is the saving real?" | Before/after scrap chart; savings math in an appendix that finance can check |
| "Will it work elsewhere?" | A slide on similarities and differences across lines, with the main risk named |
| "Who's going to do it?" | Owner and timeline stated |

## Purpose: inform, persuade, or get a decision

| Purpose | Success looks like | Line 3 example |
|---|---|---|
| Inform | audience can repeat the key point | monthly scrap update |
| Persuade | audience changes its view | convince skeptics that coolant control matters |
| Decide | a decision is made in the room | **approve $38,000** |

Write it down:

> **Purpose:** By the end of this 10-minute slot, the plant manager approves $38,000 to roll out the Line 3 fix to Lines 1, 2 and 4.

## The one-sentence message

If you can't state your message in one sentence, you are not ready to build slides. A useful pattern:

> **[What we found or did] + [why it matters to you] + [what we need from you].**

> We cut Line 3 scrap by more than half by controlling coolant and wheel dressing; the same fix on three more lines would save about $250,000 a year; we ask you to approve $38,000 today.

Test it on a colleague outside the project. If they can repeat it back, it works.

## Objectives for written documents

The same steps apply to a report, email or one-page summary. For a written document also decide: will the reader **read it before the meeting, during it, or instead of it**? A pre-read can carry more detail than a slide; an email that replaces a meeting must stand alone and put the ask in the first two lines.
