# Module 1 — Management and Motivation Principles

**Time:** 75 min

## Learning objectives

1. Summarize the main schools of management thought that shape IE practice.
2. Apply motivation theories when designing jobs and changes.
3. Plan for people and project success.

## 1. Schools of management thought

| School | Key idea | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scientific management** (Taylor) | Study work scientifically; standard methods; train workers; pay for output | Big productivity gains; measurement | Treats people as parts; separates thinking from doing |
| **Administrative management** (Fayol) | Functions of management: plan, organize, command, coordinate, control | Structure, roles | Top-down |
| **Human relations** (Mayo, Hawthorne studies) | Attention, social groups and morale affect output | People matter | Early studies' conclusions are debated |
| **Systems thinking** | Organizations are interconnected systems | Avoids local optimization | Abstract |
| **Quality / Lean management** (Deming, Ohno) | Improve the system; respect for people; workers improve their own work | Continuous improvement | Needs long-term leadership commitment |

## 2. Motivation theories

| Theory | Core idea | Design implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Maslow** — hierarchy of needs (1943) | Physiological → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualization | Safety and fair pay first; then recognition and growth |
| **Herzberg** — two-factor theory (1959) | Hygiene factors (pay, conditions) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility) create satisfaction | Fixing hygiene alone won't motivate; enrich jobs |
| **McGregor** — Theory X / Theory Y (1960) | Managers' assumptions shape behavior | Assume people want to do good work; involve them |
| **Expectancy theory** (Vroom, 1964) | Motivation = expectancy (effort → performance) × instrumentality (performance → reward) × valence (value of reward) | Achievable standards (Module 4), clear link to rewards (Module 10) |
| **Job characteristics model** (Hackman & Oldham, 1976) | Skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback | Job rotation, ownership of a whole kit, visible results |
| **Goal-setting** (Locke & Latham) | Specific, challenging, accepted goals with feedback raise performance | Hour-by-hour targets with real-time feedback |

## 3. People and project success

Most IE projects fail on people, not math. Practices that work:

- **Involve the people who do the work** in studying and redesigning it — they know where it breaks.
- **Explain the why** with data (e.g., "we miss 1 in 8 shipments because kits are short").
- **Commit that improvements won't cost jobs**; redeploy freed capacity.
- **Make standards fair and transparent** (Module 4) — a time study that feels like a speed-up will be resisted.
- **Pilot small, measure, adjust**, then expand.
- **Recognize contributions** publicly.

### Case

The kit-packing department has a history of "efficiency studies" that ended with layoffs. The IE starts by explaining the goals (stop shortages and overtime; no layoffs), asks packers what slows them down, and invites two packers to help with the time study and the layout redesign.

## Check yourself

A plant raises pay but morale and output don't improve. Which theory explains it?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Herzberg: pay is a hygiene factor — it prevents dissatisfaction but doesn't by itself motivate. Look at motivators: responsibility, recognition, meaningful work.
</details>
