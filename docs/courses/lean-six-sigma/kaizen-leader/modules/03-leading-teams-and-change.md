# Module 3 — Leading Teams, Conversations and Change

**Time:** 2.5 h

## Learning objectives

1. Lead a kaizen team through the stages of team development.
2. Use four types of conversation to move change forward.
3. Anticipate and manage resistance.

## 1. Roles

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Sponsor | Owns the business result; removes obstacles; attends kickoff and report-out |
| Process owner | Owns the process after the event; sustains it |
| Kaizen leader | Plans, facilitates, keeps the team on method and on time |
| Co-facilitator | Logistics, data, documentation, coaching |
| Team members | Bring process knowledge, do the analysis and the changes |
| Support (maintenance, IT, safety) | On call to implement changes during the week |

## 2. Team development

Bruce Tuckman's model (1965; "adjourning" added in 1977): **Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning.** In a five-day event, teams move through all stages fast; Tuesday afternoon is often the storm.

| Stage | What you see | Leader action |
|---|---|---|
| Forming | Polite, unsure, waiting for direction | Clear charter, roles, ground rules, quick win on Day 1 (gemba walk) |
| Storming | Disagreement, "this won't work here", frustration | Bring it back to data and the charter; let conflict be about ideas, not people |
| Norming | Team agrees ways of working | Delegate tasks; sub-teams |
| Performing | High energy, self-organizing | Stay out of the way; remove obstacles |
| Adjourning | Report-out, celebration | Recognize contributions; hand over to process owner |

**Ground rules** (agree on Day 1): everyone's idea counts; challenge ideas, not people; decisions on data; phones away; start and stop on time; "try it" beats "debate it."

## 3. Four conversations

Jeffrey and Laurie Ford's work on the "four conversations" of change (*The Four Conversations*, 2009) gives leaders a practical sequence:

| Conversation | Purpose | Kaizen example |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiative** | Propose what and why | "We're missing demand by 95 carriages a day. This week we'll redesign the cell to meet takt." |
| **Understanding** | Explore meaning, concerns, ideas | "What worries you about moving the test rig? What would you change?" |
| **Performance** | Make specific requests and promises: who does what by when | "Can maintenance relocate the rig by Wednesday noon?" "Yes." |
| **Closure** | Acknowledge what's done and not done; thank people; clear the deck | "The rig moved; the label printer didn't. Here's the plan for it." |

Many stalled changes are missing one: lots of initiative and understanding, but no specific requests (performance) or no closure.

## 4. Change management

- **Why change?** People need to see the gap (show the data: 465 vs 560) before they accept a solution.
- **Involve the people who do the work** — they are on the team, and they explain the change to their peers.
- **Resistance is information:** ask what problem it's pointing to (job security, past failed changes, a real flaw in the idea).
- **Job security:** state up front that improvement won't cost anyone their job; people won't improve themselves out of work. Redeploy freed capacity.
- **Make the new way easier than the old:** fixtures, layout and visual controls do more than instructions.

## Check yourself

On Tuesday two experienced operators say "we tried this five years ago." What do you do?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Ask what happened then (it's useful information), show the current data, and invite them to design a small trial ("try-storm") to test the idea this week rather than debating it.
