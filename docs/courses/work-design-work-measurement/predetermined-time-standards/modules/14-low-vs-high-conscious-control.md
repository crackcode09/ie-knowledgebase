# Module 14 — Low vs. High Conscious Control

## Objectives

- Classify gets and puts by the attention they need.
- Apply the rule that high-control activities can't be done by both hands at once.

## The idea

Some terminal activities happen almost automatically: you can drop a part in a bin while looking elsewhere. Others need the **eyes and attention** to guide the fingers: picking one washer from a pile, or starting a nut on a thread.

| | Low conscious control | High conscious control |
|---|---|---|
| Eyes | Not needed, or a glance in advance | Must focus on the target |
| Examples | Grasp an easy part; toss; loose place | Select from a jumble; tight fit; align on two pins |
| Teaching codes | G0, G2, P0, P3 | G5, P6, C2 |
| Two hands at once? | **Yes**: code the limiting hand | **No**: the hands work one after the other |

## Why it matters

1. **Time.** High-control activities take longer, and they block the other hand.
2. **Fatigue and error.** Sustained visual control is tiring, and errors happen here.
3. **Improvement.** Converting high-control work into low-control work is often the biggest saving. In the kitting example, three G5s become G2s and the hands work together (Module 20).

## Check yourself

1. Both hands each pick an easy part from separate trays (A4 G2 each). Total for the pair?
2. Both hands each pick a washer from a loose pile (A4 G5 each). Total?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Low control, so simultaneous: **6 U** (one A4 G2).
2. High control, so sequential: 2 × (4 + 5) = **18 U**. Three times longer than the first case, which is why parts presentation matters.

</details>
