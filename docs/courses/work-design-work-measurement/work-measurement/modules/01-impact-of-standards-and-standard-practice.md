# Module 01 — Impact of Standards and Standard Practice

## Objectives

- Explain how standards feed management information systems, process improvement and Lean.
- Describe a standard practice: policies and procedures that make standards trustworthy.
- Recognize the consequences of loose, tight or outdated standards.

## Where standards are used

| Use | How the standard is used |
|---|---|
| Costing and quoting | labor content = standard hours × labor rate |
| Capacity and staffing | operators = demand × standard ÷ available time |
| Scheduling | order duration = quantity × standard |
| Performance reporting | earned hours ÷ hours worked (Module 09) |
| Line balancing and Lean | element times assigned to stations against takt; standard work |
| Process improvement | before/after comparison of methods |
| Incentive pay (where used) | pay linked to output vs standard |
| Make vs buy, investment | labor savings in the business case |

Standards are **data in the management information system** — ERP routings, scheduling and cost models all depend on them.

## When standards are wrong

| Problem | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Loose** (too much time) | overstated cost, overstaffing, hidden capacity, complacency |
| **Tight** (too little time) | late orders, overtime, morale problems, distrust of all standards |
| **Outdated** (method changed) | both of the above, varying by job — reports become meaningless |
| **Inconsistent** (different analysts, different rules) | unfair comparisons between areas |

Signs: some jobs are always at 130 % efficiency and others at 70 %; planners pad schedules; nobody believes the reports.

## Standard practice

A **standard practice** (work measurement policy or manual) makes standards consistent, auditable and fair:

- **Definitions** — normal pace, qualified worker, allowances policy, what counts as standard and off-standard time.
- **Methods** — which measurement technique for which kind of work; minimum cycles; rating approach.
- **Documentation** — each standard has a study file: method description, element breakdown, data, ratings, allowances, calculations, date, analyst (Module 02).
- **Approval and communication** — who approves; how operators and supervisors are informed.
- **Maintenance** — triggers to re-study (method, equipment, material or product change); periodic audit (e.g. every 12–24 months, or when performance drifts).
- **Grievance / review** — how a disputed standard is rechecked.
- **Ethics** — tell people what is being studied and why; never study covertly; never use standards as a weapon.

## Standards and Lean

Lean uses time data differently but still needs it: **takt time** (available time ÷ demand), **cycle time** by station, and **standard work** (sequence, takt, work-in-process). Work measurement supplies the element times; Lean's focus on removing waste means standards must be **updated as methods improve** — otherwise improvements never show up in planning and cost.

## Check yourself

1. A department reports 128 % efficiency every month. What would you check first?
2. Why must the study file record the method in detail?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Whether the standards are loose or outdated (methods improved since the study), whether units are counted correctly, and whether off-standard time is being reported as on-standard.
2. Because a standard is only valid for that method; without the description, nobody can tell later whether the method has changed.
